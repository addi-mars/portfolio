Spud

01

Story

Deep in a hidden rooftop garden, something extraordinary happened. Between rows of plump radishes and carrots, something new emerged. At first, it just looked like a potato resting in the dirt. But then, with a tiny little wiggle, it stretched, unfurling its stubby legs, curly tail, and floppy ears. Its round body gave a gentle bounce, and a happy oink rang through the garden.

The same sunny afternoon, a city-dweller visited the garden. They had always found peace in tending the plants between long work hours. It was their escape from the endless noise of the city. But this creature was different—not just another plant. It was a living creature, neither pig nor potato, but something special—something soft, warm, playful.

They couldn’t take it home. It belonged to the garden, the open air, soft soil, the warm sun. They wanted to care for it, but they were too busy to be there all the time. So they did what any creative, tech-savvy person would do. From the scraps of monitors and tech they found in the garden, they built a remote care system—a way to care for this creature even when they were away.

Now it was up to them to nurture Spud, ensuring that it doesn’t leave their care during the long workday.

Inspiration

03

Sketches

  • Before Spud, I was conceptualizing some sort of alien chicken. However, I didn’t see it being animation friendly, so I scrapped it.

  • Spud’s design is fairly simple, so a lot of my sketching were concepts of poses and animations I could create. I knew I wanted several idle sequences.

  • I needed to figure out actions and setting. I originally intended to have a different menu than sleeping, and discipline rather than garden. I had a singular crude sketch of his environment, but I knew where I wanted to go from here.

04

Actions

05

Digital sketching

  • This first environment sketch had vertical lines, but it felt too streamline and rigid. The vertical orientation didn’t look very good with Spud placed on it.

  • I changed the orientation of the lines to be horizontal, but they were still missing something and just didn’t look quite right. It was too streamline.

  • I experimented with squiggled lines, which gave off more of a tilled, natural garden look. Still, the vertical orientation didn’t look great.

  • I changed the orientation of my squiggles and inverted the color scheme of it. This looked a lot better on its own and with Spud. From here, I could add vegetation.

06

UI

I explored different configurations for assets and UI. Originally, I intended for the action bar to only take up a small portion of the screen, but I opted out of that. It was important that using the program was seamless and straightforward.

I added hover states for the buttons (when clicked), because an early prototype did not have that. I made the action bar much simpler and easier to navigate, with selection states.

The health bar is a potato, which switches “bitten” states as health goes down. While a green potato doesn’t usually signify top health of the potato, the colors are an important affordance for navigating the UI. (And I think it’s a fun ironic thing that most people won’t catch anyways.)

On the tamagotchi case, there is a temperature and weather gauge that changes slowly over time. It’s the small things.

07

Main screens

  • The left-most button pulls up the main menu and navigates left. Navigate right using the button to its right. The right-most button confirms any action. Navigating past the end brings you back to the beginning, for easier and less frustrating user control.

  • A sub-menu has three options. Selecting one will either bring you to that option’s action and animation sequence, or it will bring you back to main screens if you select and confirm “exit,” which is always the right-most or last option.

  • Some actions might have a nested menu within the sub-menu, if selecting an option requires multiple steps. This menu will typically have two options, and exiting will not bring you to the main screens, but to that action’s idle.

Reflection

This project was highly interaction-driven, and I absolutely loved it. Building strong affordances was crucial—for example, using dynamic color states in the health bar, implementing intuitive hover states for buttons, and highlighting active versus inactive actions. Every interactive element was designed with intentionality, whether it directly contributed to functionality or subtly supported the overall user experience.

I leaned heavily on Figma variables and variants throughout the project, which helped streamline the complexity of the interactions. While they’re accessible on the surface, there’s a surprising depth to how variables behave, especially when layering multiple states. I picked up a lot of new skills around managing nuanced transitions and state changes, and though it was challenging at first, I quickly found a workflow that felt efficient and scalable.

One of the trickiest aspects was making interactions feel seamless. I encountered an occasional flashing effect when transitioning between videos—a common issue, based on my research and conversations with peers. It appears to stem from slight video loading delays. I also found that transition types (instant, delayed, on click, etc.) had a significant impact on the overall fluidity of the experience, and I spent a lot of time fine-tuning timing to create a cohesive flow.

I considered 3D modeling the case for the tamagotchi-inspired interface, but ultimately prioritized keeping it mobile-friendly. My goal was for the project to feel like the user is a digital companion to the city-dweller and Spud—something playable directly on a phone. That’s why, even when you put Spud to sleep, the screen remains active: the simulation can be turned off, but you can still check in on him.

I really enjoyed that this project allowed me to merge different skills and mediums.
